StealthShare · How it works

Protection is the product. Detection just saves you a keypress.

StealthShare does two separate jobs, and it is worth keeping them apart in your head. One of them is what actually keeps your screen safe. The other only decides when to switch it on for you — and it is the half that can be wrong.

The core idea

Two halves, and only one of them can miss

Always reliable

Protection — the hiding itself

Desktop icons and widgets hidden, wallpaper swapped for a neutral backdrop, chosen app windows blurred in the shared feed, notification banners silenced, risky pastes blocked.

This never depends on detection. However Privacy Mode gets turned on — your hand, the hotkey, the calendar, or auto-detect — the hiding is identical, and identical in both the App Store and direct-download builds. Nothing here is guesswork.

Best effort

Detection — the "when"

Watching for a live microphone, a live camera, a screen being shared, or a meeting on your calendar, so protection is already on before your screen is visible.

This is the part that can be wrong — it can miss a silent meeting, and it can fire when you did not want it to. Everything below is about being precise on exactly when.

So the honest summary: if you press ⌘⇧P yourself, StealthShare is deterministic. Detection is a convenience layer bolted on top of something that already works.

Three layers

How it works out that you are in a call

Three independent layers, running at the same time. They overlap on purpose — if one misses, another usually catches it.

Layer 1 · before

The meeting watcher — your calendar

Reads your calendars locally and arms Privacy Mode 1 to 30 minutes before a scheduled video call — 15 minutes by default, and you pick the number. The only layer that protects you before anything happens.

  • Counts down in the menu bar and in the popover, so you see it coming
  • Armed too early? Not now gives your screen back until the call starts
  • Holds protection through the scheduled end time
  • Works with iCloud, Google and Exchange calendars added to macOS
  • Off by default — you turn it on
Layer 2 · during

Live detection — what the Mac is doing

Checks every second whether hardware is actually live. Not whether an app is open — whether something is genuinely capturing.

  • Microphone live — about a second
  • Camera live — about a second
  • Screen being shared or recorded — about ten seconds, on purpose

The ten seconds are there so that pressing ⌘⇧4 never flips your desktop: macOS keeps its recording light on for ~3.8 seconds after any screenshot, and duration is the only thing that separates one from a real share. It only applies when you are muted with the camera off — the moment either goes live, you are protected in about a second.

Layer 3 · always

You — manual control

The fallback that cannot fail, and the one that overrides everything else. If you flip the switch yourself, StealthShare will never turn it off behind your back.

  • ⌘⇧P from any app
  • The switch in the menu-bar popover
  • Auto-detect never overrides a manual choice

What does not count as being in a call

An app simply being open — or being the window you are looking at — is not evidence of anything. Slack, Teams and Zoom sit open with a window all day between meetings. Treating that as "in a call" meant Privacy Mode stayed on permanently for anyone who leaves them running, so it does not count any more. Only live mic, live camera, or a live screen share.

A consequence worth knowing: the built-in app list and any apps you add under Custom Monitored Applications now only decide the label you see ("Zoom" instead of "Microphone in use"). They do not change whether you get detected. Detection is hardware, full stop.

The controls

Five things worth knowing exist

Everything here is reachable from the menu-bar popover or the Control Panel — and the Control Panel has its own Privacy Mode switch, so a hidden menu-bar icon never leaves you stuck.

Present Mode · on by default

Blur everything but this window

Every background window is covered; only the one you're working in stays readable. Click a different window and the clear one follows you. It's remembered between launches, so turning it off sticks.

Peek · ⌘⇧E

Reveal one window for 8 seconds

For "let me just show you this". Works even on an app from your blur list, then re-covers itself automatically so you can't forget.

Pause auto-arm

For deliberate recordings

15 minutes to 4 hours, or until you turn it back on. Everything but the last expires by itself, and it asks first — spelling out that your real desktop will be recorded.

Blur list

Yours to edit, all of it

Ships covering Slack, Mail, Messages, WhatsApp, 1Password, Bitwarden, KeePassXC, Apple Passwords, Signal and Telegram. Every entry can be switched off or deleted, including the built-in ones, and you can add any app.

Shortcuts

Both are yours to change

The Privacy Mode toggle and the peek key are click-to-record in Settings. A shortcut with no ⌘/⌥/⌃ is rejected — it would fire while you type.

Meeting watcher

Armed before the call, on your terms

Pick how early: 1, 5, 10, 15 or 30 minutes. The popover and the menu bar count down to it. If it arms sooner than you wanted, Not now hands the screen back — and it arms again when the meeting actually starts, because that is the moment that matters.

History

What armed it, and for how long

The last 100 sessions, with the trigger spelled out — "Zoom · 12 minutes", "Calendar · 34 minutes", "Turned on manually". It records what switched protection on, never what was on your screen, and it never leaves your Mac.

Always reachable

Dock icon, not just the menu bar

A crowded menu bar on a notched Mac makes macOS silently drop the icon. StealthShare sits in the Dock and ⌘-Tab too, and the Dock icon opens the Control Panel — the one route that can't be hidden.

Calendar accuracy

How well the calendar layer really works

It is not a guess or a confidence score — it is a plain matching rule, so it is either 100% for a given meeting or 0%. What matters is which of your meetings qualify.

It always arms when…

  • The invite contains a link to zoom.us, teams.microsoft.com / teams.live.com, meet.google.com, or webex.com
  • …in the event's URL field, notes, or location
  • The event is on a calendar macOS knows about, and is not an all-day event
  • You granted calendar access and switched the feature on

Meet all four and it arms every time, as far ahead as you set — 1, 5, 10, 15 or 30 minutes, 15 by default. It re-checks every 20 seconds, and immediately whenever macOS says a calendar changed, so a meeting added at the last minute is still caught.

It never arms when…

  • The meeting uses anything else — Slack huddles, Discord, Whereby, GoTo, BlueJeans, Around, or an in-house tool
  • A Teams tenant on a non-standard domain (e.g. government teams.microsoft.us)
  • There is no link at all — a phone bridge, or an in-person meeting where you still share your screen
  • The event is all-day, or the link only exists in an attachment

Realistically: high coverage if your workplace auto-inserts Zoom/Teams/Meet links (most do), near zero if it does not.

Because it is exact-match, the calendar layer is best thought of as a safety net for the muted-and-listening case, not as your primary defence. Live detection is the primary one.

Being straight with you

The limits, including the awkward ones

There is a gap — about a second, or about ten

StealthShare checks the hardware once per second. Between the instant your microphone or camera goes live and the instant protection lands, your real desktop can be on the call for up to a second — usually less.

One case is slower on purpose. If you are muted with the camera off and the only signal is the screen being captured, StealthShare waits ten seconds before believing it. macOS keeps its recording light on for about 3.8 seconds after any capture — including one ⌘⇧4 screenshot — and duration is the only thing that tells the two apart. Without the wait, every screenshot anyone took hid the desktop and swapped the wallpaper for four seconds.

Neither number can be tuned to zero: macOS gives no notification when capture begins, so the only option is to keep asking. If the gap matters to you, use the meeting watcher, or arm it yourself before you join — then there is no gap at all.

The App Store build cannot tell a camera from a screen recording

The Mac App Store sandbox blocks reading the camera directly, so that build watches the recording indicator macOS shows instead. It works — but macOS lights the same indicator for screen recording, and the two are genuinely indistinguishable without a permission StealthShare does not ask for.

Practical effect: the App Store build may say "camera or screen capture" rather than naming which. The direct-download build reads the camera directly and can tell them apart.

Recording on purpose? Pause it

Your Mac cannot tell a screencast you are making deliberately from a meeting you are sharing into — both are just "the screen is being captured". Left alone, Privacy Mode would arm and change your wallpaper in the middle of your own recording.

So there is a Pause auto-arm control in the popover. Pick how long — 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 1, 2 or 4 hours, or until you turn it back on — and detection keeps watching and reporting, but stops arming. ⌘⇧P still works throughout if you want protection anyway.

Every option except the last expires by itself, and the popover counts down the time left. That is deliberate: a pause you forget about is how you end up unprotected on your next real call.

Because pausing is the one action in StealthShare that can leave something exposed, it asks first and spells out what follows — that nothing will arm on its own, that whatever you record will show your real desktop, wallpaper and open Slack or Mail windows, that any protection StealthShare armed switches off while protection you switched on stays, and the exact time auto-arm returns. Tick "Don't ask again" once you know the drill.

The blur list only covers apps you listed

Window blurring is opt-in per app, so it fails open: an app nobody added — a password manager, a terminal, a notes app — is shown in full while StealthShare still reports that it is protecting you. The defaults now include password managers, Signal and Telegram for exactly this reason.

Present Mode inverts it. Turn it on and every background window is blurred, leaving only the window you are working in readable — so it fails closed and covers apps that were never on any list. Click a different window and the clear one follows you. Listed apps stay blurred even in front, so switching to your vault mid-call does not reveal it, and ⌘⇧E reveals the front window for 8 seconds when you genuinely want to show it.

It is deliberately manual. Engaging it by itself would mean knowing your screen is really being shared, and that cannot be separated from an ordinary call while your microphone is live — macOS lights one shared indicator for mic, camera and screen capture. Since you are almost always unmuted while sharing, an automatic version would fail exactly when it mattered.

Blurred windows are hidden from you too

A blurred window is covered on your own display, not just in the shared feed. macOS gives no way to draw one image to the screen and a different one to a capture, so anything hidden from your audience is hidden from you as well. ⌘⇧E is the escape hatch.

What it cannot hide at all

  • The macOS menu bar and Spotlight — drawn by the system, above everything
  • Other tabs inside a browser window you are deliberately sharing
  • Your webcam video and your actual room
  • The contents of held notifications — it tells you how many were held, never what they said
